Historical seismicity of the Indian subcontinent is understood as the pre-instrumental record characterized by macroseismic intensities and chronicles, as opposed to instrumental seismicity, which begins in the early twentieth century; the first systematic catalogue of Indian earthquakes is that of Oldham (1883), a posthumous edition published by the Geological Survey of India [-@Oldham1883Cachar]. The reference catalogue of the source model is SACAT (Nath, Thingbaijam & Ghosh, cited as 2011 in the paper and as 2010 in the implementation repository), homogenized to a generic $M_W$ scale, with a historical record from AD 819 and instrumental coverage through 2008, complemented by focal mechanisms from the Global CMT and paleoseismic findings [@NathThingbaijamGhosh2010Catalogo; @NathThingbaijam2012; @Ackerley2016Repo]. The seismicity map of the paper shows main events of the 819–2008 period, which implies catalogue declustering, although the algorithm and windows are not documented; the magnitude of completeness $m_c$ has spatiotemporal variation that is taken into account when estimating seismicity parameters, and the two minimum-magnitude thresholds of the model (see the source-model appendix) correspond to subcatalogues of the periods 1964–2008 and 1900–2008, with no numerical completeness intervals reported by region [@NathThingbaijam2012]. Other reference compilations are the national catalogue of the India Meteorological Department (today the National Centre for Seismology), the northern-India magnitude calibration of Ambraseys & Douglas (2004) from reassessed MSK intensities, the catalogue of 8,339 EMS-98 intensity observations for 570 earthquakes of the subcontinent and adjacent borders (1636–2009) by Martin & Szeliga (2010) — secondary citations report it as 589 events, 1636–2008, an annotated discrepancy —, the Himalayan catalogue of Szeliga et al. (2010) with events since 1762, the global GHEA/GHEC v1.0 history, the instrumental ISC-GEM catalogue (1900–2009) and the review of Himalayan historical seismicity by Bilham (2019) [@NathThingbaijam2012; @AmbraseysDouglas2004; @MartinSzeliga2010; @Szeliga2010Catalogo; @Albini2014GHEA; @Storchak2013ISCGEM; -@Bilham2019Himalaya].

In the eastern subduction interface regime, the 1762 Arakan earthquake is the only documented large pre-instrumental rupture of the Rakhine–Bangladesh subduction interface, with preferred magnitude $M_W$ 8.5 in the GHEA, reconstructions of $M_W$ 8.8, estimates of 7–8, coastal uplift of 2–2.5 m on St Martin's Island, a reported local tsunami whose existence is debated, and an estimated recurrence of 500–700 years [@Albini2014GHEA; @Cummins2007Bengal; @Wang2013Arakan; @KunduGahalaut2012; @Mondal2018Microatolls; @GuptaGahalaut2009]. At the southern edge of the region, the 2004 Sumatra–Andaman earthquake, $M_W$ 9.1 (up to 9.3 according to Stein & Okal), ruptured ~1,300–1,600 km of the subduction interface up to the Andaman–Nicobar Islands and generated the ocean-wide tsunami with 10,749 reported fatalities in India in the model table [@Lay2005Sumatra; @SteinOkal2005Sumatra; @NathThingbaijam2012].

The Himalayan interface has produced large instrumental events along the entire arc [@Bilham2019Himalaya; @NathThingbaijam2012]. Kangra 1905 ($M_S$ 7.8 ± 0.05; $M_W$ 7.8) caused about 20,000 deaths and its rupture extent remains debated between ~100 km according to geodesy and 200–350 km in the early isoseismal interpretations, marking the northwestern edge of the Central Seismic Gap [@AmbraseysBilham2000Kangra; @AmbraseysDouglas2004; @Wallace2005Kangra; @NathThingbaijam2012]. Bihar–Nepal 1934 caused about 10,700 deaths, with magnitude $M_W$ 8.1 in the intensity calibration and 8.4 in the estimate with a 5° dip, instrumental epicentre ~10 km south of Everest and a surface rupture at Sir Khola that remains disputed [@ChenMolnar1977; @MolnarDeng1984; @AmbraseysDouglas2004; @Sapkota2013Nepal; @Wesnousky2018SirKhola; @NathThingbaijam2012]. Assam–Tibet 1950, $M_W$ 8.6 in the model table (8.4–8.7 in the literature), is the largest instrumental continental earthquake of the region, with ~1,526 reported deaths in India and giant landslides damming the Brahmaputra [@ChenMolnar1977; @NathThingbaijam2012; @Bilham2019Himalaya]. Kashmir 2005 ($M_W$ 7.6, ~86,000 deaths) is the only large Himalayan earthquake with documented extensive surface rupture, ~75 km on an unusually steep (~30°N) thrust of the Hazara syntaxis [@Avouac2006Kashmir; @Pathier2006Kashmir; @Bilham2019Himalaya]. Gorkha 2015 ($M_W$ 7.8; 7.9 according to Grandin et al.) caused about 9,000 deaths through a ~140–150 km rupture of the MHT that did not reach the surface, leaving the shallow segment loaded, with a major $M_W$ 7.3 aftershock [@Avouac2015Gorkha; @Galetzka2015Gorkha; -@Grandin2015Gorkha; @McNamara2017Gorkha]. Additional crustal events of the northwestern Himalaya listed in the model table are Udayapur 1988 ($M_W$ 6.8), Uttarkashi 1991 ($M_W$ 6.8) and Chamoli 1999 ($M_W$ 6.6) [@NathThingbaijam2012].

In the intraplate regime of the northeast, the great 1897 Shillong earthquake is the largest Indian event of the early instrumental era: Richter's original $M_S$ 8.7 magnitude was revised to $M_S$ 8.0 ± 0.1, with geodetic estimates of $M_W$ 8.1 refined to $M_W$ 8.25 ± 0.1, and it constituted the first geodetic measurement of a pop-up rupture on the blind Oldham fault, documented in Oldham's (1899) monograph [@Richter1958; @Ambraseys2000Reappraisal; @BilhamEngland2001; @EnglandBilham2015; -@Oldham1899]. Other events of the domain are Cachar 1869 ($M_W$ 7.4; 7.5 according to Szeliga et al.), Dhubri 1930 ($M_W$ 7.1; 7.0 in Bilham's revision), Srimangal 1918 ($M_W$ 7.4–7.6), Bengal 1885 ($M_W$ ~7.0) and Indo-Myanmar 1988 ($M_W$ 7.2–7.5) [@Oldham1883Cachar; @AmbraseysDouglas2004; -@Szeliga2010Catalogo; @Gee1934Dhubri; @Bilham2019Himalaya; @PachecoSykes1992; @MartinSzeliga2010; @Storchak2013ISCGEM]. On the active western margin, Quetta 1935 ($M_W$ 7.7) caused ~60,000 deaths and is the largest seismic disaster of the transform margin, with the Ghazaband fault as the most probable source according to the available relocations [@NathThingbaijam2012; @EngdahlVillasenor2002].

In the peninsular stable continental region, the documented maximum is the 1819 Allah Bund (Kutch) earthquake, $M_W$ 7.7 ± 0.2 (intensity-based estimates of 7.5–8.0 and ~7.6 in the dynamic simulation), which produced an 80–90 km surface-rupture scarp with 6–9 m of uplift and the subsidence that formed the Sindri lake [@Bilham1999AllahBund; @RajendranRajendran2001Kutch; @Oldham1926AllahBund; @Sunilkumar2024AllahBund]. Bhuj 2001 appears as $M_W$ 7.7 in the model table and as 7.6 in the GEM documentation and the USGS, caused ~20,000 deaths with reverse faulting at ~24 km depth on the North Wagad fault and no primary surface rupture [@NathThingbaijam2012; @GEMHazardTeamIND; @BodinHorton2004Bhuj; @Mandal2004Bhuj]. Latur–Killari 1993 ($M_W$ 6.2, ~9,500 deaths) occurred in the Dharwar craton, in a zone then classified as minimum hazard, with a shallow hypocentre at ~2.5–6.5 km [@Gupta1993Latur; @Seeber1996Killari; @Pandey2015Latur; @NathThingbaijam2012]. Koyna 1967 ($M_W$ 6.3; 6.4–6.5 in some sources, ~177 deaths) is the reference case of the debate between tectonic origin and reservoir-associated seismicity, with seismicity persisting to the present [@GuptaRastogi1976; @Gupta1992RIS; @NathThingbaijam2012]. Jabalpur 1997 ($M_W$ 5.8; 6.0 in national sources) had an unusually deep hypocentre for the SCR (~30–35 km) on the Narmada–Son lineament, and the local versus UTC dates of Latur (1993-09-29/30) and Jabalpur (1997-05-21/22) constitute annotated discrepancies of the record [@NathThingbaijam2012; @Gupta1997NSL; @Kayal2007SCR]. Historical compilations of the shield additionally list Mahabaleshwar 1764, Damooh 1846, Mount Abu 1848, Coimbatore 1900, Son valley 1927, Satpura 1938 and Bhadrachalam 1969 [@RamalingeswaraRao1998].

The record biases are complementary and documented: for small magnitudes completeness is recent (the low-threshold subcatalogue covers only 1964–2008, versus 1900–2008 for the high-threshold one), so that detection limits the statistics of moderate events [@NathThingbaijam2012]. For large magnitudes the opposite occurs: recurrence periods (500–700 years in Arakan, 3,000–8,000 years in the Shillong Plateau) exceed the observation window, so that historical seismicity — from AD 819 in SACAT and from 1636 in the intensity compilations — is the only empirical evidence of large events in most domains, and the magnitudes assigned to large events diverge between calibrations and revisions, as shown by the annotated discrepancies for the 1762, 1869, 1897, 1934 and 1950 events [@NathThingbaijam2012; @NathThingbaijamGhosh2010Catalogo; @MartinSzeliga2010; @Mondal2018Microatolls; @BilhamEngland2001; @AmbraseysDouglas2004; @Bilham2019Himalaya].
