The reference catalog of the SAM model derives from the catalog compiled in the SARA project, built from a collection of databases and scientific publications to cover the historical and pre-instrumental periods — characterized by macroseismic intensities — and the instrumental period in a homogenized parametric catalog [@GEMHazardTeamSAM]. The updated version replaced post-1906 historical events with instrumental records, so that the 1906–1964 period is treated as part of the instrumental one, and incorporated new information from South American agencies [@GEMHazardTeamSAM]. The non-declustered version contains 106,798 events between 1513 and 2017, in the $M_W$ 3.0–9.6 range, and covers all of South America plus Panama and Trinidad and Tobago; the 1976–2017 GCMT focal mechanisms were also used for source characterization and model geometry [@GEMHazardTeamSAM]. The main global support is the ISC-GEM catalog, initially published in January 2013 for the 1900–2009 period with time-dependent cutoff thresholds — 7.5 for 1900–1918, 6.25 for 1918–1959, and 5.5 for 1960–2009 — of which the GEM regional models use an extended version built with the GEM Catalogue Toolkit and supplemented with data from local agencies [@Storchak2013ISCGEM; @DiGiacomo2018ISCGEM; @Weatherill2016Catalogues]. For the pre-instrumental period, the support is the GEM Historical Earthquake Catalogue, which gathers events with $M > 7$ between the years 1000 and 1903 and whose completeness varies widely with each region's history of settlement and documentation [@Albini2013GHEC; @GEMHazardTeamSAM]. At the regional scale, CERESIS published in 1985 a map of historical maximum intensities for the 1520–1981 period, a regional predecessor, and in 2015 a first probabilistic South American hazard assessment based on shallow and intermediate-depth historical seismicity [@Petersen2018SouthAmerica].

Magnitude homogenization is performed to $M_W$, a scale that does not saturate at high magnitudes, using local empirical relations where they exist and global relations otherwise, following the methodology of the extended ISC-GEM catalog [@GEMHazardTeamSAM; @Weatherill2016Catalogues]. The tectonic classification of the catalog — active shallow crust, interface, intraslab, and deep Bucaramanga nest, with subcategories by subduction segment — is based on hypocentral proximity to the Moho and to the interface and slab-top surfaces built with the GEM Subduction Toolkit [@GEMHazardTeamSAM]. Declustering^[Declustering removes aftershocks and dependent events from the catalog, so that the remaining population can be treated as a realization of a time-independent occurrence process [@GardnerKnopoff1974].] was applied with the Gardner and Knopoff (1974) algorithm and the Uhrhammer (1986) space–time windows, first grouping the tectonic domains that may interact — crustal, interface, and shallow slab on one side; deep slab on the other — since the algorithm compares epicentral rather than hypocentral proximities [@GardnerKnopoff1974; -@Uhrhammer1986Declustering; @GEMHazardTeamSAM]. The completeness analysis uses the Stepp (1971) method or magnitude–time histograms, and the $a$ and $b$ parameters of the doubly truncated MFDs are computed with the Weichert (1980) maximum-likelihood estimator [@Stepp1971; -@Weichert1980; @GEMHazardTeamSAM].

In the subduction interface regime (SIF), the Valdivia 1960 event ($M_W$ 9.5) is the largest instrumentally recorded earthquake and the largest known event of the southern Chile segment [@GEMHazardTeamSAM; @GEMSecretariat2015SARA; @Petersen2018SouthAmerica]. The Maule 2010 earthquake ($M_W$ 8.8) ranks among the largest of the instrumental record for the same segment [@GEMHazardTeamSAM]. The 1906 Ecuador–Colombia earthquake, published as $M_W$ 8.8 and re-estimated at 8.4, ruptured 300–500 km of the margin, and its rupture zone was partially reactivated in 1942 ($M_W$ 7.8), 1958 ($M_W$ 7.7), and 1979 ($M_W$ 8.2, Tumaco) [@KanamoriMcNally1982; @Yoshimoto2017; @Chlieh2014Ecuador]. In the Peru–northern Chile gap, the Arica 1868 ($M_W$ 8.5–9.3 estimated) and Iquique 1877 ($M_W$ 8.5–9.0 estimated) events are the largest historical events, with highly uncertain pre-instrumental magnitudes [@Carrizo2025Tsunami; @Lomnitz2004Chile]. The 1970 Ancash earthquake ($M_W$ 7.9) caused more than 65,000 fatalities, including the Huascarán debris avalanche, and the 2007 Pisco earthquake ($M_W$ 8.0) caused 593 fatalities and losses exceeding USD 1 billion [@GEMSecretariat2015SARA; @Petersen2018SouthAmerica]. Tocopilla 2007 ($M_W$ 7.7) released only ~2.5% of the moment deficit accumulated since 1877, and Iquique 2014 ($M_W$ 8.2) and Illapel 2015 ($M_W$ 8.3) were partial ruptures relative to their gaps [@BejarPizarro2010Tocopilla; @Carrizo2025Tsunami].

In the intraslab regime (SIS), the 1939 Chillán earthquake ($M_S$ 7.8, intermediate depth ~80–100 km) involved normal faulting within the subducting slab [@Beck1998Chile]. The 2005 Tarapacá earthquake ($M_W$ 7.8) was a large intermediate-depth event (90–115 km) with normal faulting accommodating down-dip extension on a subhorizontal plane [@DelouisLegrand2007; @Peyrat2006Tarapaca]. In the deep-focus category, the 1970 Colombia earthquake ($M_W$ 8.2, ~650 km) and the 1994 Bolivia earthquake ($M_W$ 8.2–8.3, 631–647 km) were in their time the two largest instrumentally recorded deep-focus earthquakes; the Bolivia event remains the largest recorded in South America and, globally, was surpassed only in May 2013 by the Sea of Okhotsk earthquake ($M_W$ 8.3, ~609 km) [@Antolik1996Bolivia; @USGS1994Bolivia].

In the active shallow crust (ASC), the 1944 San Juan earthquake ($M_W$ 7.0) is the largest natural disaster in Argentine history (~10,000 fatalities), with rupture in the La Laja zone, eastern Precordillera, and a reverse mechanism [@AlvaradoBeck2006; @GEMSecretariat2015SARA]; San Juan seismicity shows documented crustal recurrence in 1894, 1944, 1952 ($M_W$ 6.8), and 1977, with surface deformation in 1944 and in the 1977 Caucete sequence ($M_W$ 7.4), which produced extensive liquefaction and scarps of secondary normal faults [@AlvaradoBeck2006; @KadinskyCade1985; @Tectonics2021LasChacras]. On the Caribbean margin, the 1967 Caracas earthquake ($M_W$ 6.6) caused 240 fatalities and illustrates the margin's risk through concentration of exposure [@GEMSecretariat2015SARA]. In Colombia, Popayán 1983 ($M_W$ 5.5–5.6) was a destructive crustal event and Quindío 1999 ($M_W$ 6.2) caused more than 1,000 fatalities and losses on the order of 2% of GDP [@Mayorga2016Coulomb; @GEMSecretariat2015SARA; @Petersen2018SouthAmerica]. In Ecuador, the Riobamba–Guamote sequence and the 1949 Ambato earthquake ($M_W$ ~6.5) belong to the canonical crustal record [@Petersen2018SouthAmerica], and in Bolivia the 1998 Aiquile–Totora event ($M_W$ 6.6, 95–105 fatalities) characterizes the Subandean regime [@GEMSecretariat2015SARA]. For the stable continental region (SCR), the 1986–1989 João Câmara intraplate sequence — more than 14,000 events in 18 months, fifteen of them with magnitude greater than 4, and a mainshock of $M_W$ 5.1 — is the reference for Brazilian intraplate seismogenic behavior, and the 2007 Itacarambi earthquake ($m_b$ 4.9) produced the first recorded earthquake fatality in Brazil, in the São Francisco craton [@Takeya1989JoaoCamara; @USGS1986JoaoCamara; @Chimpliganond2010Itacarambi].

The pre-1900 portion of the catalog is highly incomplete and contains uncertain locations and magnitudes for few selected events, which makes it difficult to estimate rates from it, although it may prove relevant in site-specific analyses [@Petersen2018SouthAmerica]. The completeness of the global historical catalog depends on each region's history of settlement and documentation [@Albini2013GHEC]. The record's biases are complementary: instrumental detection limits completeness at small magnitudes, while the ~120-year instrumental window is short compared with the recurrence of large interface events — on the order of ~400 years for the characteristic events of the southern Chile segment, according to the 1575–1960 comparison and the slip budget against convergence rates — so historical seismicity constitutes the only empirical evidence of the largest events in much of the region [@Petersen2018SouthAmerica].
