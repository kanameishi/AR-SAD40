The Indian subcontinent moves northward relative to Eurasia and collides with the southern Asian margin at a rate of 35–45 mm/yr, an ongoing process since the Eocene that has uplifted the Himalaya, the Tien Shan, the Pamir and the Tibetan Plateau [@GEMHazardTeamIND; @MolnarTapponnier1975]^[Slip and convergence rates are given in mm/yr (millimetres per year); the equivalence in geological units is 1 mm/yr = 1 m/ka, i.e. 1 km/Ma (kilometres per million years).]. The Indian crust, more rigid than the Asian continental crust, concentrates deformation and seismicity in the Himalayan arc and to its north, outside the national territory; the documented exceptions are the northwestern and northeastern Himalayan segments within Indian borders and the Shillong region, where part of the convergence is accommodated on intraplate faults [@GEMHazardTeamIND]. Of the total convergence budget, the Himalayan system absorbs approximately half: ~18–21 mm/yr of shortening across the front in Nepal, ~10–14 mm/yr in the Indian northwestern Himalaya, 12.5 ± 1 mm/yr of oblique convergence in Kashmir and ~20–25 mm/yr in the eastern Himalaya [@Bilham1997GPS; @LaveAvouac2000; @Ader2012MHT; @BanerjeeBurgmann2002; @Schiffman2013Kashmir; @Banerjee2008Bhutan; @McQuarrie2014Bhutan]. The present-day stress field is dominated by the NNE–SSW to N–S compression imposed by the collision, consistent with plate motion (~N25°E relative to Eurasia according to MORVEL56-NNR), and along the Himalayan front the shortening direction rotates along the arc while remaining nearly perpendicular to it [@Argus2011MORVEL; @Heidbach2018WSM; @BanerjeeBurgmann2002]. The current collision regime has been in place since the Eocene, with long-constant shortening rates since the middle Miocene along the Himalayan front [@MolnarTapponnier1975; @McQuarrie2014Bhutan].

The basal slip interface of the Himalayan orogenic wedge is the Main Himalayan Thrust (MHT)^[Basal décollement over which the Indian lithosphere underthrusts the chain; it emerges at the surface as the Main Frontal Thrust (MFT), with the Main Boundary Thrust (MBT) and the Main Central Thrust (MCT) to the north [@Ader2012MHT; @BanerjeeBurgmann2002].] of the fold-and-thrust belt. The MHT is locked from the surface to ~18 km depth in the central Himalaya, with a transition zone to aseismic slip between 18 and 25 km, and the interseismic coupling map of the full arc quantifies a slip deficit of 18.5 ± 1 mm/yr [@Ader2012MHT; @StevensAvouac2015Coupling].^[Interseismic coupling is the degree of locking of a fault interface between earthquakes, which determines the rate of slip-deficit accumulation [@StevensAvouac2015Coupling].] The secular slip rate of the Holocene MFT is 21 ± 1.5 mm/yr in central Nepal, 19 ± 6 mm/yr in western Nepal and 9 ± 3 mm/yr in northwestern India [@LaveAvouac2000; @Mugnier2003MFT; @Wesnousky1999MFT]. Documented seismic gaps persist, in particular the ~600–800 km Central Seismic Gap between the Kangra 1905 and Bihar–Nepal 1934 ruptures, and an accumulated moment deficit along the arc equivalent to several $M_W$ ≥ 8 earthquakes [@Khattri1987Gap; @BanerjeeBurgmann2002; @Bilham2001Science]. Paleoseismology shows that the MFT is an emergent system that has produced surface ruptures in large medieval events, with documented extents exceeding 240 km in Nepal [@Kumar2006Paleoseis; @Lave2005Medieval]. The Himalayan thrusts represent the largest source of seismic hazard for India according to the model documentation, given that earthquakes in Nepal, Bhutan and Pakistan can produce dangerous seismic shaking in densely populated northern India [@GEMHazardTeamIND].

To the east, the India plate subducts obliquely beneath the Burma/Sunda microplate along the Rakhine–Bangladesh subduction interface and the Andaman trench, with India–Sunda motion of ~35 mm/yr toward N11°E at 22°N and ~46 mm/yr highly oblique overall [@Socquet2006Myanmar; @Steckler2016IndoBurman; @Maurin2018Tesis]. The convergence is partitioned between shortening across the Burma plate (12–24 mm/yr), dextral shear on the Sagaing fault (~18–21 mm/yr) and ≤8–10 mm/yr on the Churachandpur–Mao fault [@Mallick2019IndiaBurma; @Vigny2003Sagaing; @Steckler2016IndoBurman]. Geodetic models indicate that the Rakhine–Bangladesh subduction interface is coupled and accumulates 13–17 mm/yr of convergence, with more than 5.5 m of accumulated deficit over ~400 years, sufficient for an $M_W$ 8.2–9 event [@Steckler2016IndoBurman; @Mallick2019IndiaBurma]. At the southern end, the Sumatra–Andaman subduction interface produced the 2004, $M_W$ 9.1 earthquake at the edge of the model region [@Lay2005Sumatra; @NathThingbaijam2012]. The western boundary of the plate is the left-lateral Chaman transform system, with ~18 ± 1 mm/yr measured by GPS, lower and heterogeneous segmented geodetic and InSAR estimates (6.8–10.3 mm/yr at Chaman, 13.4–16.9 mm/yr at Ornach–Nal) and long-term geological rates of 19–35 mm/yr [@Mohadjer2010Chaman; @Szeliga2012Chaman; @Beun1979Chaman; @Lawrence1992Chaman; @UlHadi2013Chaman]. The Sulaiman–Kirthar fold belt accommodates transpressive convergence of ~5 ± 1 mm/yr north of Quetta, decreasing southward (2.5–5 mm/yr), and the model documentation additionally identifies the Hindukush–Pamir subduction in the northwest as a seismotectonic province [@Szeliga2012Chaman; @NathThingbaijam2012].

The Shillong Plateau (Meghalaya) is an Archean basement block elevated ~2 km that functions as a pop-up structure bounded by two conjugate reverse faults: the Oldham fault, blind, with ~110 km of trace and SSW dip on the northern edge, and the Dauki fault, with ~194 km of surface trace and northward dip on the southern edge [@BilhamEngland2001; @Verma2022Shillong]. The 1897 earthquake ruptured the Oldham fault with 16 m of reverse slip in the best fit (25 ± 5 m in the later refinement, $M_W$ 8.25 ± 0.1) between 9 and 45 km depth, and the combined horizontal shortening of both faults is 4 ± 2 mm/yr since the Plio-Pleistocene [@BilhamEngland2001; @EnglandBilham2015]. Peninsular India, a Precambrian shield of cratons, basins and the Deccan Traps province, is classified in the model as a stable continental region (SCR)^[Stable continental region: an intraplate domain of low deformation rate away from active plate boundaries [@NathThingbaijam2012].], although the strong Indian crust transmits compressive stresses over large distances and has produced large destructive earthquakes well within its borders, such as Bhuj 2001 ($M_W$ 7.6–7.7) [@NathThingbaijam2012; @GEMHazardTeamIND; @BodinHorton2004Bhuj]. SCR seismicity concentrates in reactivated zones of weakness: the Mesozoic Kutch rift basin, with inverted E–W faults (Allah Bund, Island Belt, Kachchh Mainland, Wagad, Katrol Hill, Nagar Parkar), and the Narmada–Son lineament, a Proterozoic ENE–WSW zone of weakness along which at least four $M$ > 5.4 earthquakes have occurred [@Biswas1987Kutch; @RajendranRajendran2001Kutch; @Srivastava2025Kutch; @Gupta1997NSL]. Koyna–Warna seismicity sustains a debate between tectonic and reservoir-induced origin, and the Latur–Killari 1993 event ($M_W$ 6.2) occurred in the Dharwar craton, in a zone then classified as minimum hazard [@GuptaRastogi1976; @Gupta1992RIS; @Kayal2007SCR; @Gupta1993Latur; @Seeber1996Killari].

The adopted source model is that of Nath & Thingbaijam (2012), implemented in OpenQuake as the GEM IND model version v2012.2.0 by N. Ackerley (Natural Resources Canada) in collaboration with GEM, with seismogenic zonation derived from Thingbaijam & Nath (2011) [@NathThingbaijam2012; @GEMHazardTeamIND; @ThingbaijamNath2011; @Ackerley2016Repo]. The source characterization comprises three models — a single set of uniform-seismicity area seismogenic zones and two gridded smoothed-seismicity models — combined as alternative branches of the logic tree (see the source-model appendix) [@NathThingbaijam2012]. The area zones were delineated in four depth bands, from the shallow crust to deep subduction, from seismicity, fault patterns and focal-solution similarity, with one representative mechanism per zone derived from the weighted average of moment tensors [@ThingbaijamNath2011; @NathThingbaijam2012].
