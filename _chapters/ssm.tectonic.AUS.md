The Australian continent consists of ancient continental crust, far from active plate boundaries, with relatively low seismicity rates, and is classified as a stable continental region (SCR), in the same category as central and eastern North America [@Johnston1994SCR; @SchulteMooney2005; @AllenEtAl2023NSHA23]. The proximity of the northern plate margin, however, conditions the hazard landscape: the Banda Sea lies about 400 km from the north coast, the seismic energy of its earthquakes propagates efficiently through the low-attenuation North Australian Craton, and historical damage in Darwin from large earthquakes of that region has been documented [@HearnWebb1984Banda; @McCue2013Darwin; @AllenEtAl2023NSHA23; @AllenEtAl2024NSHA23Data]. In this context, the Australian national model treats damaging earthquakes in low-seismicity regions as low-probability, high-consequence events [@AllenEtAl2023NSHA23].

Australia's current crustal stress field is compressive and has been in force for approximately 5 Ma; the orientation of the maximum horizontal stress $S_{Hmax}$ controls the reactivation potential of faults, which reactivate preferentially in a reverse regime when perpendicular to that orientation [@HillisReynolds2003; @SandifordEtAl2004; @RajabiEtAl2017StressField; @RajabiEtAl2017Modelo3D]. This ~5 Ma time window constitutes the operative neotectonic framework of the model: in the Nullarbor Plain, for example, the long-term uplift rate of a fault is estimated as the greater of the scarp height divided by the ~5 Ma of the current stress regime and the long-term erosion rate [@DickinsonEtAl2002; @SandifordEtAl2004; @Clark2023FSM23]. The regional exception to this compressive pattern is the Northwest Shelf, where the crustal stress regime is predominantly strike-slip, with reverse and normal components at the bends of the major fault systems [@WhitneyEtAl2016NWS; @AllenEtAl2023NSHA23; @Clark2023FSM23].

The Neotectonic Domains model divides the continent into classes according to the age and deformation history of the crust [@ClarkMcPhersonVanDissen2012]. In Australia, the seismic cycles of a given fault are typically thousands to tens of thousands of years or more, and there is evidence for a class of large earthquakes on unextended cratonic faults that are essentially one-off events, without recurrence on the timescales relevant to PSHA [@ClarkMcPhersonVanDissen2012; @ClarkAllen2018; @KingEtAl2019Rupturas; @KingEtAl2021Petermann; @ClarkEtAl2020LakeMuir]. The neotectonic domains^[The neotectonic domain groups regions with comparable fault behaviour and deformation rates over recent geological time; in NSHA23 it is used to assign common recurrence parameters to the zones of each class [@ClarkMcPhersonVanDissen2012; @AllenEtAl2023NSHA23].] are a formal component of the source characterization model: zones are classified by domain, catalogue events are aggregated by class and the computed b value is applied to all zones of that class, in addition to parameterizing the uncharacterized faults of the fault source model [@ClarkMcPhersonVanDissen2012; @AllenEtAl2023NSHA23; @AllenEtAl2024NSHA23Data]. The b values derived from the $M_W$-homogenized catalogue (up to 1.363) exceed the 0.75–1.0 range proposed in earlier studies (Brown and Gibson, 2000), a difference that the model documentation attributes to the non-linear $M_L$–$M_W$ relation [-@BrownGibson2000; @AllenEtAl2023NSHA23].

The structural exception to this framework is the Western Australia Shear Zone (WASZ), an active fault-and-fold system that runs along the reactivated passive margin of the Northwest Shelf through the Browse, Roebuck and Carnarvon basins, reoccupying Paleozoic and Mesozoic rift structures with present-day reactivation of dominant dextral component (Whitney and Hengesh, 2013, cited in [@Clark2023FSM23]; also [@HengeshWhitney2016WASZ; -@WhitneyEtAl2016NWS]). This reactivation responds to the difference in convergence rate between the Australian Plate and the Sunda arc west of 120°E (66–72 mm/a) and between the Australian Plate and the Banda arc collision zone east of 120°E (24–39 mm/a) [@HengeshWhitney2016WASZ; -@WhitneyEtAl2016NWS]^[Slip and convergence rates are given in mm/a (millimetres per year); the equivalence in geological units is 1 mm/a = 1 m/ka, i.e. 1 km/Ma (kilometres per million years).]. The system is organized into an inner platform fault zone, an outer platform zone and an inner basin zone [@HengeshWhitney2016WASZ; -@WhitneyEtAl2016NWS; @Clark2023FSM23].

Since sources outside the continent contribute significantly to hazard in Australia and its territories, the model includes source zones in the high-seismicity regions of Indonesia and Papua New Guinea, with the main subduction interface and crustal faults parameterized from the regional assessments of Papua New Guinea and Indonesia and from the global tsunami hazard assessment [@IrsyamEtAl2017; @GriffinDavies2018PlateMargin; @GhasemiEtAl2020PNG; @DaviesEtAl2017Tsunami; @AllenEtAl2023NSHA23; @AllenEtAl2024NSHA23Data]. The northern margin source model is coupled to all continental models in the ensemble [@AllenEtAl2024NSHA23Data].

The tectonic region types (TRT)^[The TRT (tectonic region type) is the label that defines the seismogenic regime of each source and determines which ground motion models are applied to it [@AllenEtAl2023NSHA23].] of the model are Cratonic, Non-Cratonic, Extended Crust, Oceanic Crust, Shallow Active Crust (plate margin), Subduction Interface and Subduction Intraslab [@AllenEtAl2023NSHA23; @AllenEtAl2024NSHA23Data]. For ground motion characterization (GMCM) they are collapsed into three trees: Cratonic; Non-cratonic, which absorbs Extended, Oceanic and Shallow Active Crust with the justification that the energy from the northern margin travels efficiently through the low-attenuation North Australian Craton; and Subduction, which combines interface and intraslab [@AllenEtAl2023NSHA23].
