The neotectonic activity criterion adopted in the fault databases underlying the model is not a single standard: in the NAAFD, activity is assessed case by case from published Quaternary evidence, geomorphology and geodesy; the GAF-DB documents this delegation of the criterion to each regional compiler, without a uniform global time window, prioritizing regional accuracy over global consistency; in East Africa, the Macgregor database classifies activity by Cenozoic epochs as an interpretive atlas, and in southern Malawi the SMAFD applies explicit Quaternary geomorphological criteria [@PoggiEtAl2020; @StyronPagani2020GAFDB; @Macgregor2015EAR; @Williams2021SMSSD].

The reference database for North Africa is the NAAFD (GEM North Africa Active Fault Database, Styron and Poggi), a public compilation of about 125 active fault traces mapped on 30 m SRTM topography from literature, seismicity and geodesy, in which approximately half of the structures come from published field studies and slip rates^[Slip rate: average rate of relative displacement accumulated on a fault, commonly expressed in mm/yr. [-@StyronPoggi2018NAAFD]] were estimated for all of them, by expert judgment on geodetic, seismic or geomorphological data when no published value existed; the repository is `github.com/GEMScienceTools/n_africa_active_faults`, with DOI 10.13117/N-AFRICA-ACTIVE-FAULTS [-@StyronPoggi2018NAAFD; @PoggiEtAl2020]^[Slip and convergence rates are given in mm/yr (millimetres per year); the equivalence in geological units is 1 mm/yr = 1 m/ka, i.e. 1 km/Ma (kilometres per million years).]. At the global scale, the GAF-DB (GEM Global Active Faults Database, v2019.0, Styron and Pagani) harmonizes 13,628 structures from 19 regional datasets, 77 % with rate information; for Africa it includes the NAAFD and the Macgregor East Africa dataset, and its authors declare sub-Saharan Africa a priority area for future mapping because of the differences in style and metadata of the latter [-@StyronPagani2020GAFDB; @Macgregor2015EAR]. The Seismotectonic Map of Africa (Meghraoui et al., RMCA Tervuren/CGMW) also exists as a continental cartographic reference [-@Meghraoui2016MapAfrica].

In the Atlas, the North Atlas and South Atlas faults of the western High Atlas are moderately inward-dipping reverse faults, with rates of 0.1–0.5 mm/yr and zones of 250–500 km capable of large events; the western segment of the South Atlas fault is associated with the 1960 Agadir earthquake [@Meghraoui1999; @PoggiEtAl2020]. The faults of the Middle Atlas and the Midelt basin are reverse-sinistral with rates of 0.05–0.5 mm/yr, and the thrusts of the Ouarzazate basin record a shortening of ~0.1 mm/yr [@PoggiEtAl2020]. The Rif faults, sinistral-reverse in the south and southwest, reverse in the west and normal in the east, reach ~1 mm/yr on segments of about 150 km, with the 2004 Al Hoceima event as recent expression [@PoggiEtAl2020]. The Tell Atlas faults, reverse to reverse-dextral under ENE transpression and with rates of ~1 mm/yr, produce crustal events up to $M_W$ 7.1: the El Asnam fault ruptured 36 km in 1980 with paleoseismic evidence in trenches, and the Sahel anticline is being uplifted at 0.84–1.2 mm/yr, quantified after the 2003 Boumerdes event [@PoggiEtAl2020; @Meghraoui1988; @Maouche2011]. The offshore thrusts of the Maghreb, known only from marine geophysics and without measured rates, would accommodate at least half of the ~5 mm/yr of convergence [@PoggiEtAl2020]. In Tunisia and eastern Algeria, the Zaghouan system and the Aurès combine NE–SW sinistral strike-slip faults with normal faults in grabens and a shortening of ~0.1 mm/yr in the southern foreland [@PoggiEtAl2020]. In Libya, the Hun graben contains the only structures in the country with confirmed Quaternary activity and hosted the 1935 event ($M_L$ 7.1) [@Suleiman2004Libia; @PoggiEtAl2020]. In northeastern Egypt, the normal faults of the Gulf of Suez show rates of 0.5–1 mm/yr versus ~0.1 mm/yr at Wadi Araba, and the Kalabsha fault at Aswan is the probable source of the 1981 event associated with the Nasser reservoir [@PoggiEtAl2020].

In the EARS the documented canonical structures include the Kanda fault (Ufipa plateau, Tanzania), a 160 km active normal fault and candidate for the source of the 1910 Rukwa event; the Livingstone fault (Malawi rift), a ~120 km border fault with a ~2 km scarp and a total throw of ~6 km, although the 2009 Karonga sequence ruptured faults of its hanging-wall block; and the Machaze fault (Mozambique), a steep normal fault dipping ~70–75°, a probable reactivation of a pre-existing structure, with a maximum slip of ~4.5 m at 10–15 km depth [@Vittori1997Kanda; @DelvauxBarth2010Stress; @Poggi2016SSAHARA; @Biggs2010Karonga; @Copley2012Machaze; @FentonBommer2006Machaze]. The Aswa shear zone links the eastern and western branches of the rift and hosted the 1990 Sudan sequence [@GirdlerMcConnell1994Sudan]. For southern Malawi there are no directly measured slip rates; the SMAFD/SMSSD database of Williams et al. estimated them by partitioning the geodetic extension over 23 traces, with intermediate rates of ~0.05–0.8 mm/yr (border faults up to 0.6–0.9 mm/yr), full-rupture magnitudes of $M_W$ 5.6–7.8 and recurrences of $10^2$–$10^5$ years on border faults and of $10^3$–$10^6$ years on intra-basin faults, a database later than the model and incorporated into recent versions of the GAF-DB [-@Williams2021SMSSD; @StyronPagani2020GAFDB].

The agreement with the model is structurally distinct between the two components. In the north, the fault sources of the model derive directly from the NAAFD, and slip rates appear only in the chapter's external evidence for the named faults (e.g. FL_45 El Asnam: 0.6 [0.5–1.0] mm/yr vertical) [@PoggiEtAl2020; @StyronPoggi2018NAAFD]. In the south, the SSA component contains no fault source: no EARS structure (Kanda, Livingstone, Machaze nor the Macgregor systems) is represented as a fault source, and the rift hazard rests entirely on smoothed seismicity, even though the literature documents border faults capable of $M_W$ 7–8 [@Poggi2016SSAHARA; @Biggs2010Karonga; @Williams2021SMSSD].
