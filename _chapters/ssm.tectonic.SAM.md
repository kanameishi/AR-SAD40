South American seismicity arises mainly from the subduction of oceanic lithosphere beneath the continent's western margin and from the eastward translation of the Caribbean plate along the northern margin: in the west, the Nazca plate subducts beneath the central and northern parts of the continent, while the Antarctic plate subducts beneath the southern part [@GEMHazardTeamSAM]. Nazca subduction occurs at the Peru–Chile trench and has produced several of the largest recorded earthquakes, including Valdivia 1960 ($M_W$ 9.5) and Maule 2010 ($M_W$ 8.8) [@GEMHazardTeamSAM]. Nazca–South America convergence is among the fastest on the planet: classical geodetic estimates give ~66 mm/yr with azimuth ~N78°E, and recent geodetic models predict a maximum rate of 65.5 ± 0.8 mm/yr near 30°S, decreasing to 50.8 ± 0.7 mm/yr off northern Colombia [@Angermann1999; @Vigny2022Nazca]^[Slip and convergence rates are given in mm/yr (millimetres per year); the equivalence in geological units is 1 mm/yr = 1 m/ka, i.e. 1 km/Ma (kilometres per million years).]. The NUVEL-1A and MORVEL geologic models predict rates ~1 cm/yr higher than the geodetic ones along the Chilean margin, so the choice of plate-motion model modifies the convergence assigned to the interface by up to ~10 mm/yr [@Vigny2022Nazca]. Along the northern margin, relative Caribbean–South America motion is ~20 mm/yr eastward and is concentrated in the right-lateral strike-slip fault system of northern Venezuela; off eastern Venezuela, that motion is accommodated in the Lesser Antilles subduction zone [@PousseBeltran2016ElPilar; @Higgins2021; @GEMHazardTeamSAM]. East and south of the Andes, the continent may be considered stable, although stresses transmitted across the South American shield produce seismicity in Brazil and neighboring countries at rates that are a small fraction of those of the active margins [@GEMHazardTeamSAM].

Upper-plate deformation associated with subduction built the Andes and remains distributed across the entire Andean zone, accommodated in large part by large, fast-slipping reverse faults in the Subandean zone, at the eastern front of the cordillera, although oblique subduction and gravitational forces also generate strike-slip and normal faulting over much of the Andes [@GEMHazardTeamSAM]. The Nazca plate has three documented flat-slab subduction segments: the Bucaramanga segment in Colombia, the Peruvian segment, and the Pampean segment in Chile–central Argentina [@RamosFolguera2009; @WagnerOkal2019Pucallpa]. The Pampean flat slab (approximately 27–33°S) extends horizontally ~200–300 km beneath the southern central Andes, is widely associated with subduction of the Juan Fernández Ridge, and frames the back-arc deformation of Argentina: the Precordillera, an east-vergent thin-skinned fold-and-thrust belt, to the west, and the Sierras Pampeanas, thick-skinned basement uplifts, to the east [@RamosFolguera2009; @CahillIsacks1992; @RimandoSchoenbohm2020]. The Peruvian flat slab is associated with subduction of the Nazca Ridge; beneath central Peru the plate descends at a normal dip to ~70–100 km and then flattens for more than 400 km before resuming its descent [@WagnerOkal2019Pucallpa]. The two gaps in the Quaternary Andean volcanic arc coincide with the Peruvian and Pampean flat slabs [@RamosFolguera2009; @CahillIsacks1992]. The present-day stress field of the South American plate is predominantly E–W to ENE–WSW compressive in the east of the continent, as inferred from the focal mechanisms of Brazilian intraplate seismicity [@Chimpliganond2010Itacarambi].

The interface of the Nazca subduction zone is segmented from southern Chile to northern Colombia by the trench intersection of bathymetric structures and fracture zones, among them the Juan Fernández, Iquique, Nazca, and Carnegie ridges [@GEMHazardTeamSAM]. The model also includes subduction geometries for the northern Panama deformation belt and for the Lesser Antilles, the latter inherited from the CCARA/CCA project [@GEMHazardTeamSAM]. The Peru–northern Chile segment constitutes a long-recognized seismic gap whose last great earthquakes occurred in 1868 and 1877; the 2001 ($M_W$ 8.4) and 2014 ($M_W$ 8.2) events ruptured it only partially [@Carrizo2025Tsunami].

The intraslab regime (SIS) corresponds to seismicity within the downgoing plate [@GEMHazardTeamSAM]. This regime includes anomalous concentrations of intermediate-depth seismicity: the Bucaramanga nest (~6.8°N, 73.1°W), the world's densest concentration in that depth range, occupies a volume of ~800 km³ at 150–170 km and produces approximately 60% of the events recorded by the Colombian National Seismological Network; recent work proposes lithospheric delamination as the mechanism of the lower cluster and dehydration embrittlement for the upper one [@PerezForero2023Bucaramanga]. Alongside it, the Pucallpa nest (central Peru, 140–180 km), which marks the northern edge of a depression in the Peruvian flat slab, and the Pipanaco nest (central Argentina) are documented, making three nests associated with flat slabs in South America [@WagnerOkal2019Pucallpa]. Deep-focus seismicity (>300 km) has produced two events with $M_W$ > 8 in the region: the 1970 deep Colombia earthquake ($M_W$ 8.2, ~650 km) and the 1994 Bolivia earthquake ($M_W$ 8.2–8.3, 631–647 km), the latter the largest deep earthquake recorded in South America [@Antolik1996Bolivia; @USGS1994Bolivia].

The active shallow crust (ASC) comprises the reverse-faulting systems of the Andean front and the back-arc — Precordillera, Sierras Pampeanas, Subandean zone — the intra-arc and forearc strike-slip systems — Liquiñe–Ofqui, Atacama, San Ramón, Magallanes–Fagnano — and the strike-slip system of the Caribbean margin — Boconó, San Sebastián, El Pilar, Oca–Ancón [@GEMHazardTeamSAM; @Santibanez2019ChileFaults; @Veloza2012ATA]. Eastern and central South America — the Brazilian shield and the cratons — constitute the model's stable continental region (SCR); there, seismicity concentrates in inherited zones of weakness from the Brasiliano belts between cratons and produces moderate-magnitude sequences, such as João Câmara 1986 and Itacarambi 2007, with a historical maximum magnitude of $m_b$ 6.2, lower than that of other intraplate regions such as eastern North America, Australia, or India [@Chimpliganond2010Itacarambi; @Takeya1989JoaoCamara].

The version history of the SAM model comprises v2016.0.0 (first version, SARA project), v2018.0.0 (new Nazca subduction model and revision of faults in Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia), v2018.1.0 (update of CCA-LAN sources), v2020.0.0 (new faults in Colombia, Ecuador, Argentina, and Chile, with revised completeness), and v2020.1.0 ($M_{\min}$ extended to $M$ 4 for distributed crustal seismicity, consolidated intraslab sources, and updated GMM logic tree) [@GEMHazardTeamSAM].
