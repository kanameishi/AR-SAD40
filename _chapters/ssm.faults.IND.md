The activity criterion adopted in the published practice of the region is the standard Quaternary one: a structure is considered active when there is evidence of Quaternary or neotectonic deformation — scarps, displaced terraces and fans, trench paleoseismology and luminescence (OSL) or cosmogenic dating — applied in a documented manner in the Himalayan front and in the Kutch rift [@Kumar2006Paleoseis; @Wesnousky1999MFT; @Mathew2006KMF; @Kothyari2016SWF]. The applicable databases are HimaTibetMap-1.0, an inventory of active structures of the Indo-Asian collision used in the mapping of the model implementation repository; the seismotectonic atlas of the Geological Survey of India, the fault base used in the model zonation together with the USGS map of Afghanistan, the Wellman (1966) compilation, the Curray (1991) tectonic map of Andaman–Sumatra and the He & Tsukuda (2003) fault map of Tibet; and, across the board, the global GEM GAF-DB database, which incorporates the structures of the region in a homogeneous schema [@Styron2010HimaTibet; @Ackerley2016Repo; @Dasgupta2000Atlas; @NathThingbaijam2012; @WheelerRukstales2007; @Wellman1966; @Curray1991Andaman; @HeTsukuda2003Tibet; @StyronPagani2020GAFDB].

The principal structure of the region is the MHT–MFT system, the basal thrust (décollement)^[Décollement: basal detachment surface over which the orogenic wedge slides; in the Himalaya it emerges at the surface as the Main Frontal Thrust [@Ader2012MHT; @BanerjeeBurgmann2002].] that runs the ~2,500 km of the Himalayan arc and emerges at the Main Frontal Thrust, with published Holocene slip rates of 21 ± 1.5 mm/yr in central Nepal, 19 ± 6 mm/yr in western Nepal and 9 ± 3 mm/yr in northwestern India, and an interseismic slip deficit of 18.5 ± 1 mm/yr across the whole arc [@LaveAvouac2000; @Mugnier2003MFT; @Wesnousky1999MFT; @StevensAvouac2015Coupling]^[Slip and convergence rates are given in mm/yr (millimetres per year); the equivalence in geological units is 1 mm/yr = 1 m/ka, i.e. 1 km/Ma (kilometres per million years).]. The paleoseismic evidence includes a large medieval rupture (~AD 1100) of more than 240 km in Nepal, extendable to 600–800 km, and the 1505 event ($M_W$ 8.2–8.9) [@Kumar2006Paleoseis; @Lave2005Medieval; @Bilham2019Himalaya]. The internal MBT and MCT thrusts concentrate the partitioning of Miocene exhumation, the MCT being essentially inactive in the Late Quaternary, while present-day shortening is absorbed almost entirely by the MFT; in the Hazara syntaxis, the Balakot–Bagh fault, a steep (~30°N) reverse branch of the system, produced in 2005 the only documented extensive surface rupture of a large Himalayan earthquake, ~75 km long with mean slip of ~4 m [@Whipp2007Himalaya; @Avouac2006Kashmir; @Pathier2006Kashmir].

In the east, the Rakhine–Bangladesh subduction interface offers ~700 km of potential rupture with coupled convergence of 13–17 mm/yr, marine terraces documenting three coseismic uplifts and a 500–700 year recurrence derived from the 1762 event [@Steckler2016IndoBurman; @Mallick2019IndiaBurma; @Wang2013Arakan; @Mondal2018Microatolls]. The dextral shear of the oblique system is partitioned between the Sagaing fault (~18–21 mm/yr, ~1,200 km) and the Churachandpur–Mao fault (≤8–10 mm/yr) [@Vigny2003Sagaing; @Mallick2019IndiaBurma; @Steckler2016IndoBurman]. On the western margin, the Chaman fault, a left-lateral transform, shows ~18 ± 1 mm/yr by GPS, segmented geodetic rates of 6.8–10.3 mm/yr and long-term geological rates of 19–35 mm/yr; the transpressive Sulaiman–Kirthar belt accommodates ~5 ± 1 mm/yr of convergence decreasing southward, and the Ghazaband fault is the candidate identified as the source of the 1935 Quetta earthquake [@Mohadjer2010Chaman; @Szeliga2012Chaman; @Beun1979Chaman; @Lawrence1992Chaman; @UlHadi2013Chaman; @EngdahlVillasenor2002].

The Shillong Plateau is bounded by two conjugate reverse faults with published rates: the Oldham fault, blind, ~110 km long with a southward dip of 45–57°, with ~2.5 mm/yr of slip, and the Dauki fault, emergent, ~194 km long with a northward dip of ~50°, whose combined shortening is 4 ± 2 mm/yr since the Plio-Pleistocene, with an alternative estimate of vertical displacement of 0–0.68 mm/yr for Dauki [@BilhamEngland2001; @Vernant2014Shillong; @Verma2022Shillong; @Biswas2007Shillong]. The Oldham fault ruptured in 1897 with 16–25 m of slip between 9 and 45 km depth and raised the northern edge of the plateau by more than 11 m, which, at the published rate, implies recurrences of 3,000–8,000 years for events of that type; the Dauki has no attributed historical ruptures, although its active geomorphology is clear and scenarios up to $M$ 8.0 have been published [@BilhamEngland2001; @EnglandBilham2015; @BhutanScenarios2020].

In peninsular India, the Allah Bund fault, an E–W reverse fault of the Kutch rift, produced in 1819 an 80–90 km scarp with 6–9 m of deformation in a single event, although quantitative slip rates are scarce [@RajendranRajendran2001Kutch; @Bilham1999AllahBund; @Sunilkumar2024AllahBund]. The Kachchh Mainland fault, an E–W reverse fault on the southern boundary of the Wagad uplift, shows active fold growth documented by luminescence; the North Wagad fault, identified by the aftershocks as the source of Bhuj 2001, and the South Wagad fault, ~80 km long with a 40 m scarp, documented reactivation in the 12–3 ka interval and uplift of 2.8 mm/yr over 12 ka, complete the system, in a rift where GPS records active compression of ~0.05 $\mu$strain/yr [@Mathew2006KMF; @Mandal2004Bhuj; @Kothyari2016SWF; @Gahalaut2019Kutch]. The Narmada–Son lineament, a reactivated Proterozoic ENE–WSW zone of weakness, has produced at least four historical $M$ > 5.4 events, including Jabalpur 1997 ($M_W$ 5.8, ~30–35 km depth), Son valley 1927 and Satpura 1938, and the Koyna–Warna zone records ten $M$ ≥ 5 events in ~50 years of persistent seismicity [@Gupta1997NSL; @RamalingeswaraRao1998; @NathThingbaijam2012; @Gupta1992RIS]. No robust published Quaternary slip rates exist for most peninsular faults, a gap acknowledged by the cited literature [@Kothyari2016SWF; @Srivastava2025Kutch].

As documented in the source-model appendix, the IND model contains no fault sources [@NathThingbaijam2012; @GEMHazardTeamIND]. Three observations from the literature delimit the context of this modelling decision: none of the great historical Himalayan earthquakes produced documented primary surface rupture until 2005; the main structures (MHT, Oldham) are largely blind; and the most damaging SCR events (Bhuj 2001, Latur 1993) occurred on faults not previously recognized as seismogenic [@Kumar2006Paleoseis; @SeeberArmbruster1981; @BilhamEngland2001; @Ader2012MHT; @BodinHorton2004Bhuj; @Gupta1993Latur]. Consequently, the structures described appear in the model only through the zonation and the smoothed seismicity, and the maximum magnitude and recurrence of known structures (MFT, Oldham, Dauki, Allah Bund) are not anchored to fault geometries in the model [@NathThingbaijam2012; @GEMHazardTeamIND].
