The neotectonic activity criterion adopted by the SAM model corresponds to a Quaternary–Late Pleistocene time window: the fault compilation emphasized structures with slip rates equal to or greater than 0.1 mm/yr — published or estimated with quantitative and semiquantitative criteria — evidence of Late Pleistocene tectonic activity, confirmed or suspected, and status as confirmed or suspected sources of earthquakes with $M_W \geq 5.5$ [@GEMHazardTeamSAM]^[Slip and convergence rates are given in mm/yr (millimetres per year); the equivalence in geological units is 1 mm/yr = 1 m/ka, i.e. 1 km/Ma (kilometres per million years).]. The database derives from a compilation initiated before the SARA project by South American geologists, based on project II-2 of the International Lithosphere Program, the Multinational Andean Project, and national neotectonic databases, and completed by the GEM team with local experts through 2020 [@GEMHazardTeamSAM; @Costa2003ILP]. The compilation was published as the SARA Active Fault Database, with DOI 10.13117/SARA-ACTIVE-FAULTS, a public GitHub repository, and a CC-BY-SA 4.0 license [@Alvarado2017SARAFaults]. At the global scale, the GEM Global Active Faults Database integrates SARA together with other regional compilations and contains ~13,500 faults, of which ~77% have slip-rate information [@StyronPagani2020GAFDB]. For northwestern South America there is also the open-access Active Tectonics of the Andes archive [@Veloza2012ATA]. The national inventories of Quaternary faults and folds compiled in the 2000s — Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, and Venezuela — were later reviewed in a continental synthesis that acknowledges that knowledge of South American Quaternary faults remains incomplete [@Petersen2018SouthAmerica; @Costa2006Overview].

On the Caribbean margin of Venezuela and northern Colombia, the ~1,600 km right-lateral strike-slip Boconó–San Sebastián–El Pilar–Los Bajos system accommodates the ~20 mm/yr Caribbean–South America relative motion between the strike-slip system and the eastern Caribbean subduction, with a mean Quaternary rate of ~10 mm/yr [@Veloza2012ATA]. The Boconó fault has a preferred right-lateral rate of 5.0–11.2 mm/yr derived from dated Pleistocene markers in the Yaracuy valley, with earlier estimates of 7–10 mm/yr in the Venezuelan Andes and a southward decrease from Mérida toward San Cristóbal [@PousseBeltran2017Bocono]. The El Pilar fault accommodates the full Caribbean–South America strike-slip motion (20–22 mm/yr from GPS); dense measurements indicate up to ~50% aseismic slip in some segments, and InSAR observations document shallow creep^[Creep, or aseismic slip, is the continuous movement of a fault without earthquake generation; it reduces the fraction of tectonic slip available to accumulate elastic deformation [@PousseBeltran2016ElPilar].] of ~13 to ~40 mm/yr with along-strike variation [@PousseBeltran2016ElPilar]. The Oca–Ancón system, a right-lateral strike-slip system over 400 km long between Venezuela and northern Colombia, has an overall paleoseismic rate of ~1.9 ± 0.3 mm/yr obtained from trenches, versus earlier geomorphic estimates of 2.5–4.0 mm/yr [@Veloza2012ATA; @Audemard1996Oca]. The Santa Marta–Bucaramanga system is a left-lateral strike-slip system with ~6 ± 2 mm/yr according to early GPS measurements cited secondhand, and the San Sebastián fault, in north-central Venezuela, shows a geodetic ~16.4 ± 1.7 mm/yr [@Symithe2015Caribbean; @Higgins2021].

In the southern Andes, the Liquiñe–Ofqui fault zone — a ~1,000–1,200 km intra-arc right-lateral strike-slip zone — shows Holocene rates of 18.8 ± 2.0 mm/yr on an individual strand, accommodating ~82% of the trench-parallel component of convergence, and of 13.8 ± 4.9 to 14.1 ± 1.0 mm/yr in Patagonia, with vertical components of ~6 ± 3 mm/yr; the absence of historical events with $M_W > 6.5$ in the system implies creep or multi-branch $M_W$ ~7 events with recurrence intervals longer than 150 years [@AstudilloSotomayor2021Liquine; @DePascale2025LOFZ; @Santibanez2019ChileFaults]. The Atacama fault system, in the Coastal Cordillera of northern Chile (~1,000 km between 21° and 25°S), is a reactivated Mesozoic left-lateral structure with slow Quaternary rates — ~0.2 mm/yr long-term on the Mejillones fault, ~0.07 mm/yr at Salar del Carmen, and 0.2–0.6 mm/yr from InSAR at Salar Grande — and with documented coseismic reactivation during large subduction events [@Gonzalez2019Mejillones; @Schmidt2025SalarGrande; @Ewiak2025Atacama]. In central Chile, the San Ramón fault, at the mountain front of Santiago, has rates of ~0.3 mm/yr, and the Magallanes–Fagnano system, a left-lateral strike-slip system of southern Patagonia, ~6.9 ± 1.3 mm/yr in Argentina [@Santibanez2019ChileFaults; @Sandoval2024Magallanes]. Overall, Chilean crustal fault rates range from ~0.2 mm/yr in the forearc to ~7 mm/yr in the intra-arc, with recurrence intervals of 200 to 50,000 years for ~$M_W$ 7 events [@Santibanez2019ChileFaults].

The Precordillera and Sierras Pampeanas of Argentina (27–33°S) concentrate close to 50% of the country's documented Quaternary deformation, in the Andean Orogenic Front, with GPS shortening rates of 2–7 mm/yr [@Frontiers2022Marquesado]. Published Quaternary slip rates include ~1.1 mm/yr on the La Laja fault, ~0.5 mm/yr at La Rinconada, 0.9–1.5 mm/yr at Las Tapias — a segment of the Villicum–Zonda–Pedernal thrust — 0.27 ± 0.11 mm/yr at Las Peñas, ~0.3–0.5 mm/yr of shortening at eastern Pie de Palo, ~0.2 mm/yr of shortening and ~0.3–0.4 mm/yr of uplift in the Las Chacras fault zone, and ~0.7 mm/yr on the Los Molinos branch of the Comechingones system [@Tectonics2021LasChacras]. The paleoseismic and surface-deformation evidence of the 1944 ($M_W$ 7.0) and 1977 ($M_W$ 7.4) events supports an estimated primary surface-rupture threshold of $M_W > 7.0$ for this compressive environment [@Tectonics2021LasChacras; @KadinskyCade1985]. In the Subandean zone of Bolivia and northwestern Argentina, Quaternary shortening of the southern belt is 7–13 mm/yr from balanced geologic cross-sections; geodetic modeling of the décollement indicates loading rates of ~14 mm/yr on the northern profile and ~7 mm/yr on the southern one, with locking widths of ~100 and ~61 km respectively [@Weiss2016Subandes]. The wedge-front thrust system accommodates at least half — and locally all — of that rate in a 20–40 km band with one to four thrust faults, and it has been proposed that an event rupturing the full locked width of the décollement could exceed $M_W$ 8.5 with a mean recurrence on the order of a thousand years [@Weiss2015MTFS; @Brooks2011Backarc].

For comparison, the USGS 2017 model includes only 26 crustal faults with published rates — under the criterion of an annual event rate equal to or greater than 1/2475 and $M \geq 6.8$ — among them Boconó, El Pilar, Oca–Ancón, San Sebastián, Santa Marta–Bucaramanga, San Ramón, Lago Fagnano, Cordillera Blanca, and Pallatanga, whereas the earlier USGS 2010 model contained more than 100 faults, mostly slowly deforming with rates assigned by category [@Petersen2018SouthAmerica]. Both the regional model and the USGS models treat most slow or poorly studied structures as seismogenic background [@Petersen2018SouthAmerica; @GEMHazardTeamSAM]. In Ecuador, the Quito fault shows high geodetic rates without clear geologic corroboration, so the USGS model represents it through compressional zones rather than as a single fault [@Petersen2018SouthAmerica].
