Neither the ARB model documentation nor that of the MIE model declares a formal neotectonic criterion —an activity time window or an evidence standard for fault inclusion— so the criterion in force in regional compilations is de facto evidence of Quaternary and geodetic activity [@GEMHazardTeamARB; @Meghraoui2015DSF; @StyronPagani2020GAFDB]. The EMME project compiled an active-fault-section database for the Middle East that was used to parametrize the fault branch of the MIE model [@Gulen2014EMMEFaults; @GEMHazardTeamMIE; @Danciu2017EMMESources]. The current cross-cutting reference is the GEM Global Active Faults Database (GAF-DB), with about 13,500 faults carrying geometry, kinematics, and slip-rate attributes —around 77% with rate information— under a CC-BY-SA 4.0 license [@StyronPagani2020GAFDB]. For the peninsula proper, the ARB model relies exclusively on area sources, so the faults documented in this section constitute tectonic reference rather than model sources; the model's only fault sources, in the western Zagros, are described in the source-model chapter [@GEMHazardTeamARB].

The Dead Sea Transform (DST) is a left-lateral transform system more than 1000 km long between the Red Sea and eastern Anatolia, with ~105 km of displacement accumulated since the middle Miocene and a mean locking depth of ~11 km along the system [@Wechsler2014DSF; @Meghraoui2015DSF]. On the Gulf of Aqaba segment the GNSS geodetic rate is 4.5–4.7 ± 0.2 mm/yr left-lateral [@ArRajehi2010ArabianPlate]^[Slip and convergence rates are given in mm/yr (millimetres per year); the equivalence in geological units is 1 mm/yr = 1 m/ka, i.e. 1 km/Ma (kilometres per million years).]. In Wadi Araba, geomorphology and cosmogenic dating of alluvial deposits offset over the last ~11–140 ka yield rates of 5.4 ± 2.7 mm/yr and 2–6 mm/yr [@LeBeon2010WadiAraba; @Klinger2000Araba]. In the Jordan Valley, paleoseismology documents 12 events in 14 ka with rates of ~5 mm/yr over the last 47.5 ka, and on the Jordan Gorge segment the minimum rate is 3 mm/yr over the last 5 ka [@Ferry2011JordanValley; @Marco2005JordanGorge]. In the Lebanese restraining bend, the Yammouneh fault shows 5.1 ± 1.3 mm/yr measured on offset alluvial fans, whereas the Roum fault records 0.86–1.05 mm/yr in paleoseismic trenches spanning the last 10.5 ka and the Rachaya–Serghaya system 1.4 ± 0.2 mm/yr in the Holocene [@Daeron2004Yammouneh; @Nemer2006Roum; @Gomez2003Serghaya; @Nemer2008Rachaya]. In Syria, the Missyaf fault reaches 4.9–6.3 mm/yr from the Al Harif Roman aqueduct offset over 4300 years [@Meghraoui2003Missyaf; @Sbeinati2010Missyaf]. The northern segment (Ghab–Karasu) maintains a persistent discrepancy between geodesy (2.2–2.5 mm/yr) and geologic–archaeological markers (4–6 mm/yr) [@Meghraoui2015DSF; @Li2024DSF]. The paleoseismic record of the system as a whole shows episodic behavior and temporal clustering of large events [@Ferry2011JordanValley; @Wechsler2014DSF].

In the Zagros, the Mountain Front Fault is a segmented, mostly blind thrust on which almost all nucleated earthquakes are shallow (<20 km); the 2017 Sarpol Zahab event ($M_W$ 7.3) nucleated in its basement without surface rupture [@Talebian2004Zagros; @Nissen2019SarpolZahab]. The High Zagros Fault and the Main Zagros Reverse Fault are the main structures of the High Zagros, partially exposed, and the Zagros Foredeep Fault is a minor foreland structure [@Nissen2011Zagros]. The Main Recent Fault is a right-lateral strike-slip fault with a finite displacement of ~50–60 km and an estimated geologic rate of 10–17 mm/yr —if its initiation is placed 3–5 Ma ago— versus 3 ± 2 mm/yr from present-day geodesy; the 1909 Dorud earthquake ($M_S$ 7.4) is associated with this structure [@Talebian2002MRF; @Vernant2004IranGPS; @Authemayou2006Zagros; @Tchalenko1974Dorud]. Total shortening across the Zagros measured by GPS is 3–6 mm/yr in the west and 6–10 mm/yr in the east [@Vernant2004IranGPS; @Allen2013Zagros].

The Makran megathrust is a low-angle interface (~11° mean dip in the geodetic coupling model), locked at least to ~38 km depth according to geodetic inversions, with coupling varying along strike —higher on the eastern segment— and an estimated potential of up to $M_W$ ~8.8 [@Penney2017Makran; @Frohling2016Makran].^[Seismic coupling: the fraction of interplate slip that accumulates as elastic deformation and is released in earthquakes, as opposed to aseismic slip [@Penney2017Makran].] The anchor event of the structure is the 1945 earthquake ($M_W$ 8.1) of the eastern segment [@Byrne1992Makran]. The transition between Zagros collision and Makran subduction is accommodated by the Minab–Zendan and Ornach–Nal transfer zones, to the west and east respectively [@Regard2005Minab].

In the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden, the ridges and transforms of the rift system are submarine structures with no published local slip rates; only plate-opening rates exist, from ~7 mm/yr in the northern Red Sea to ~17 mm/yr in the south, and 15–23 mm/yr from west to east in the Gulf of Aden, where the Sheba Ridge with the Alula–Fartak and Socotra–Hadbeen transforms accommodates right-lateral strike-slip [@ArRajehi2010ArabianPlate; @Viltres2022ArabianPlate]. The Owen fracture zone continues the boundary in the Indian Ocean with right-lateral strike-slip of 2.5–3.2 mm/yr [@ArRajehi2010ArabianPlate]. In the peninsular interior, the harrats host normal faults associated with dike intrusion, documented at Harrat Lunayyir in 2009 with a graben more than 10 km long and up to ~1 m of vertical throw [@Pallister2010Lunayyir; @Jonsson2012InSAR]. Outside these provinces, the interior of the peninsula has no documented active faults with published rates in the compilations consulted, consistent with the stable-continental-region character of the plate and with the representation of the ARB model exclusively through area sources [@ArRajehi2010ArabianPlate; @GEMHazardTeamARB].
