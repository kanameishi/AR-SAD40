Historical seismicity is understood as that recorded before the instrumental era, characterized by macroseismic intensities and documented chronicles, in contrast to the instrumental seismicity of the twentieth century onward; in the XAF region the depth of the macroseismic record is highly uneven: the Maghreb preserves documented events from 408 AD in Utica and 856 AD in Tunis, with the 1183 event that destroyed Tripoli causing more than 20,000 deaths, whereas the GEH global historical catalogue (1000–1903, $M>7$) contributes only eight events to the entire sub-Saharan region, a lack that the model authors attribute to the absence of historical records in sub-Saharan and central Africa and that they declare as a completeness problem capable of biasing large-magnitude rates [@PoggiEtAl2020; @Suleiman2004Libia; @Poggi2016SSAHARA]. Ambraseys and Adams estimate that the 1900–1930 record south of 20°N is complete only down to approximately $M_S$ 6 and that the pre-1900 record will probably remain incomplete for all magnitudes [-@AmbraseysAdams1991Africa].

The reference catalogue of the northern component is the GEM-NAEC, homogenized to $M_W$, with 5,170 events of $4 \le M_W \le 8.5$ for the period 1016–2013, built by merging ISC-GEM, ISC-Reviewed, GCMT, the catalogue of the Spanish IGN, the European-Mediterranean EMEC and the GEM-GHEC global historical catalogue [@PoggiEtAl2020; @GEMHazard2023NAFdoc; @Albini2014GHEA; @GrunthalWahlstrom2012EMEC]. The merge applied solution prioritization and duplicate detection with epoch-dependent time-space windows, and the magnitude conversion used global relationships: the $M_S$ and $m_b$ relations of Weatherill et al., the polynomial $M_L$ relation of Edwards et al., and $M_D$ in 1:1 equivalence with high assigned uncertainty; the regional data were insufficient to calibrate local conversion rules [@PoggiEtAl2020; @Weatherill2016Catalogues; -@Edwards2010Mw]. The occurrence analysis was performed on the declustered catalogue^[Declustering: removal of foreshocks and aftershocks to obtain a catalogue of independent events on which to estimate stationary occurrence rates. [@GardnerKnopoff1974]] following the procedure of Poggi et al. (2017), that is, the Gardner and Knopoff algorithm, an attribution inferred from the explicit referral of the model documentation [@PoggiEtAl2020; @Poggi2017EAR; -@GardnerKnopoff1974].

The catalogue of the sub-Saharan component, SSA-GEM, merges ISC-Reviewed (26,322 events from 1904–2013 in the regional window), ISC-GEM v3 (285 regional events), GCMT (614), the GEH historical catalogue and three regional AfricaArray catalogues of small magnitudes ($M_L$ 1.3–4.4) used mainly for spatial patterns [@Poggi2016SSAHARA]. The conversions employ the global relationships of Weatherill et al. for ISC and NEIC, Edwards et al. for the AfricaArray $M_L$, and an assumed equivalence of the Zimbabwe bulletin Mblg with $M_L$ with an arbitrary uncertainty of 0.3 [@Poggi2016SSAHARA; @Weatherill2016Catalogues]. After duplicate removal with a 0.5°/120 s window and Gardner and Knopoff declustering, the catalogue was reduced to 7,259 events out of the original 29,803, with $3 \le M_W \le 7.53$; completeness was estimated with the Stepp method in its HMTK implementation, manually refined on the magnitude-frequency distribution by region and by zone [@Poggi2016SSAHARA; -@GardnerKnopoff1974; @Stepp1971]. The global instrumental ISC-GEM catalogue acts as the backbone for $M_W \ge 5.5$ in both submodels, with native GCMT $M_W$ magnitudes from 1976–77 onward [@Storchak2013ISCGEM; @DiGiacomo2015ISCGEM; @DiGiacomo2018ISCGEM; @Ekstrom2012GCMT].

By seismogenic regime, the canonical events of the EARS include the Rukwa earthquake (Tanzania, 13 December 1910, $M_S$ 7.4), the largest documented on the continent, with severe damage on the shore of Lake Tanganyika and aftershocks of $M_S$ 5.7 and 6.0, possibly attributed to the Kanda fault [@Ambraseys1991b; @AmbraseysAdams1991Africa; @Vittori1997Kanda; @Hodge2015Malawi]; the Subukia earthquake (Kenya, 6 January 1928, $M_S$ 6.9), with 38 km of surface rupture and a maximum offset of 2.4 m [@Ambraseys1991a]; the Juba sequence (Sudan, May–July 1990), with the largest instrumental sub-Saharan event ($M_W$ 7.2) on the Aswa shear zone [@GirdlerMcConnell1994Sudan]; the Lake Tanganyika earthquake (5 December 2005, $M_W$ 6.8) [@Poggi2016SSAHARA]; the Machaze earthquake (Mozambique, 22 February 2006, 22:19 UTC, $M_W$ 7.0), the first mapped surface rupture in southern Africa, with 15 km observed and vertical offset of 0.4–2.05 m [@FentonBommer2006Machaze; @Copley2012Machaze]; and the Karonga swarm (Malawi, December 2009, four events of $M_W$ 5.5–6.0 with combined seismological moment equivalent to $M_W$ 6.3), which ruptured shallow normal faults with no prior geomorphic expression on the hanging wall of the Livingstone fault [@Biggs2010Karonga; @Kolawole2018].

In the northern ASC regime the prominent events include Agadir (Morocco, 29 February 1960, $M_W$ 5.8–5.9), the deadliest earthquake of Morocco with 12,000–15,000 deaths despite its moderate magnitude, associated with the western segment of the South Atlas fault [@CherkaouiElHassani2012; @Paradise2005; @Meghraoui1999; @Storchak2013ISCGEM]; El Asnam (Algeria, 10 October 1980, $M_W$ 7.1), the largest instrumental event of the Maghreb, with about 36 km of surface rupture and paleoseismic evidence in trenches [@Ouyed1983; @Ambraseys1981; @Meghraoui1988; @PoggiEtAl2020]; Boumerdes–Zemmouri (May 2003, $M_W$ 6.8), a large coastal thrust event with coseismic uplift of the Sahel anticline [@MeghraouiPondrelli2012Atlas; @Maouche2011]; Al Hoceima (Morocco, 2004, $M_W$ 6.3–6.4, 629 deaths) [@CherkaouiElHassani2012; @MeghraouiPondrelli2012Atlas]; and Cairo–Dahshur (Egypt, 1992, $m_b$ 5.8, 561 deaths) [@Sawires2015; @Hussein2013Egypt; @PoggiEtAl2020]. As evidence of intraplate hazard in the SCC regime stand the Guinea earthquake (22 December 1983, $M_W$ 6.3), the largest intraplate event of West Africa with about 10 km of surface rupture and about 300 deaths [@Langer1987Guinea; @Dorbath1984Guinea], and the Hun graben earthquake (Libya, 1935, $M_L$ 7.1), the largest instrumental Libyan event in a domain considered stable [@Suleiman2004Libia; @PoggiEtAl2020]. The Al Haouz event (High Atlas, 8 September 2023, $M_W$ 6.8–6.9, about 2,900 deaths), the largest of the Atlas belt, postdates the model catalogue cutoff (2013) and is not incorporated into its rates [@Yeck2023AlHaouz].

The authors themselves declare as documented biases the incompleteness of the sub-Saharan historical record for large magnitudes, the scarcity of strong-motion recordings across the whole region —which prevents calibrating or validating regional attenuation relationships— and particularly critical data in Libya [@Poggi2016SSAHARA; @PoggiEtAl2020]. In the opposite direction, the recent Machaze and Karonga events occurred on structures with no clear prior geomorphic expression, which warns against interpreting the absence of historical seismicity as the absence of hazard [@FentonBommer2006Machaze; @Biggs2010Karonga].
