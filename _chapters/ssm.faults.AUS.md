The adopted neotectonic activity criterion is the deformation accumulated under the current stress regime, in force for approximately 5 Ma, operationally expressed in the *Australian Neotectonic Features Database* (ANFD/NFD), which compiles on the order of 400 faults [@Clark2012NFD; @SandifordEtAl2004; @AllenEtAl2023NSHA23]. The neotectonic features extracted from the database were geometrically simplified following the rules of Clark et al. (2016) to generate the fault source model [-@ClarkEtAl2016FSM18; @AllenEtAl2023NSHA23]. Where available, slip rates are assigned from paleoseismic studies; for most structures they are estimated by analogy with faults studied in similar neotectonic regimes, with constraint from the local rates of landscape modification by erosion or deposition [-@ClarkEtAl2016FSM18; @AllenEtAl2023NSHA23; @AllenEtAl2024NSHA23Data]. Unless the scarp-forming earthquake occurred in the historical era, the assignment of activity rates to the fault sources does not depend on the model's seismicity catalogue [@AllenEtAl2023NSHA23].

The FSM23 fault source model includes 441 sources (418 onshore and 23 offshore), modelled as simplified planes defined by surface trace, dip, dip direction and slip rate [@ClarkEtAl2016FSM18; @Clark2023FSM23; @AllenEtAl2024NSHA23Data]. Dips come preferentially from seismic reflection profiles [@YangEtAl2022Nullarbor; @SellmannEtAl2023Nullarbor] or are inferred from geology, geomorphology and analogous settings; the base of the seismogenic rupture is taken as the regional maximum seismogenic depth of the distributed seismicity model [@AllenEtAl2024NSHA23Data]. The rupture area is computed from fault length with the Leonard (2010, 2014) scaling relations [@Leonard2010Escalado; @Leonard2014Escalado; @Clark2023FSM23]. The MFDs^[The MFD (magnitude–frequency distribution) is the frequency distribution of magnitudes that characterizes the seismic recurrence of a source [@YoungsCoppersmith1985].] of the fault sources were modelled as Gutenberg–Richter, Youngs and Coppersmith characteristic, or periodic maximum magnitude, and the rates of the three models were collapsed into an incremental MFD by weighted average with weights from the expert elicitation [@GutenbergRichter1944; -@YoungsCoppersmith1985; @GriffinEtAl2018Elicitacion; @AllenEtAl2024NSHA23Data].

The structures with published paleoseismic evidence are distributed across the neotectonic domains of the continent. In the Yilgarn Craton of southwestern Western Australia, the Meckering fault, which produced the 1968 rupture of 37–44 km with up to 2.5 m of displacement, has a maximum rate bounded at ≤0.2 m/Ma from basement erosion (Clark and Edwards, 2018, cited in [@Clark2023FSM23]; [@CroneEtAl1997Episodicidad; @CroneEtAl2003Paleosismicidad; @KingEtAl2019Rupturas]); the Hyden fault documents repeated Quaternary events [@CroneEtAl2003Paleosismicidad] and the Dumbleyung fault has paleoseismic evidence (Estrada, 2009, cited in [@Clark2023FSM23]). In central Australia, the Tennant Creek faults have maximum per-segment rates of 4–9 m/Ma [@CroneEtAl1992Tennant; @CroneEtAl1997Episodicidad; @KingEtAl2019Rupturas]; the Marryat Creek fault, in the Musgrave Block, ~1 m/Ma at most [@CroneEtAl1997Episodicidad; @KingEtAl2019Rupturas]; the Petermann fault, ~0.7 m/Ma at most, with the 2016 scarp of studied geomorphic longevity [@GoldEtAl2019Petermann; @KingEtAl2021Petermann]; and the Pukatja (Ernabella) fault, ~1.2 m/Ma at most [@ClarkEtAl2014Ernabella; @KingEtAl2019Rupturas]. The Lake Muir fault, in the southwest, combines paleoseismic and geodetic evidence [@ClarkEtAl2020LakeMuir].

In the south and southeast, the Willunga fault, in the Mt Lofty Ranges, reaches ~80 m/Ma of vertical uplift at most (lower bound of 50 m/Ma), and the Alma fault ~25 m/Ma at least [@Sandiford2003Neotectonica; @Clark2023FSM23]; the Mt Lofty–Flinders range-front reverse fault system (Bremer, Burra, Norwest, Meadows, Mt Torrens, Eden-Burnside, among others) shows rates of 0.02–0.17 mm/a (20–170 m/Ma) per fault, with an unconfirmed estimate of 0.7 mm/a [@Sandiford2003Neotectonica; @QuigleyEtAl2006AustraliaSurCentral]^[Slip and convergence rates are given in mm/a (millimetres per year); the equivalence in geological units is 1 mm/a = 1 m/ka, i.e. 1 km/Ma (kilometres per million years).]. The Lake Edgar fault, in southwestern Tasmania, records three events in ~60 ka with 8 m of cumulative uplift [@ClarkEtAl2011LakeEdgar; @Clark2023FSM23], and the Lort River fault, in southeastern Western Australia, three events in ~60 ka with 2.5–3 m of cumulative uplift (Estrada, 2009, cited in [@Clark2023FSM23]). In the Murray Basin, the Cadell fault documents temporally clustered morphogenic seismicity, of episodic character, without a consolidated numerical slip rate in the consulted sources [@ClarkEtAl2015Cadell]; the Pliocene dune ridges of the basin bound the regional rates (Sandiford, 2003b, estimates 10–15 m/Ma of slip and a relief-preservation threshold of ~3 m/Ma) [@Clark2023FSM23; @Sandiford2003Neotectonica]. In the Northwest Shelf, the WASZ faults are parameterized with slip vectors H:V = 5 for the major platform faults and H:V = 1 for those oblique to $S_{Hmax}$; the Dampier fault reaches 80 m/Ma vertical and the Barrow fault ~24 m/Ma vertical [@HengeshWhitney2016WASZ; @WhitneyEtAl2016NWS; @Clark2023FSM23]. The Nullarbor Plain scarps (Madura, Mundrabilla, Forrest, among others) are parameterized from seismic reflection and digital elevation models, with the scarp height divided by the ~5 Ma of the stress regime as a minimum rate of 4 m/Ma [@YangEtAl2022Nullarbor; @SellmannEtAl2023Nullarbor; @Clark2023FSM23].

The integration of the fault sources with the distributed seismicity is described in the source-model chapter; in Australia there is no demonstrated link between independent instrumental events and mapped neotectonic faults (Balfour et al., 2015, cited in [@AllenEtAl2023NSHA23]), so the impact of double counting is considered minor [@AllenEtAl2023NSHA23; @AllenEtAl2024NSHA23Data]. Not all ~400 ANFD faults are represented in FSM23, since the simplification rules exclude small or poorly characterized features, and the attribution of instrumental events to specific faults remains exceptional and not the norm [@AllenEtAl2023NSHA23]. The differences between FSM18 and FSM23 include parameterization changes in a moderate number of features, geometric modifications in a few, and additions such as the Torquay fault in the Otway Basin and three new segments of the Inner Shelf Fault Zone after the 2019 Broome event; the rate changes in the Northwest Shelf significantly increased the regional moment release rate [@Clark2023FSM23; @AllenEtAl2023NSHA23].
